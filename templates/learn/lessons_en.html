The stage: the tropical Pacific

Meet the Pacific

El Niño happens in the equatorial Pacific, a band more than 15,000 km long between Indonesia and South America. Normally the ocean is tilted: very warm water piles up in the west while cold water wells up off Peru and Ecuador.

  • The western warm pool is above 28 °C, the largest body of warm water on the planet.
  • The thermocline separates warm surface water from cold deep water: ~200 m deep in the west, ~50 m in the east.
  • Ocean and atmosphere are coupled: air rises and rains over warm water and sinks, dry, over cold water.

ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation) is the scientific name. It has an ocean part, El Niño and La Niña, and an atmospheric part, the Southern Oscillation: a pressure seesaw between Tahiti and Darwin (Australia) described by Gilbert Walker in the 1920s.

The name comes from fishermen in northern Peru, who in the late 19th century called the warm current that appeared around Christmas the "corriente del Niño" (the Christ child's current). Today it is reserved for years when that warming is much stronger and longer.

Trade winds and upwelling

Wind and upwelling

The trade winds push surface water west, where sea level ends up about 50 cm higher than off Ecuador. In the east, the water pushed away is replaced by cold water rising from below: upwelling.

  • More wind, more tilt: a deeper thermocline in the west and a shallower one in the east.
  • Upwelling brings nutrients to the surface and feeds plankton.
  • That is why Peru's sea supports the world's largest single-species fishery: the anchoveta.

Off Peru two effects add up. Southerly winds blowing along the coast turn surface water offshore because of Earth's rotation (Ekman transport), and deep water rises to replace it. At the equator the trades spread water north and south, with the same result.

The Humboldt Current also carries cold water up from the south. That cold water chills the air above it, keeps it stable and explains why Peru's coast is a desert despite lying in the tropics.

How an El Niño starts

How it starts

It starts when the trade winds weaken, often through westerly wind bursts over the western Pacific. The piled-up warm water is released and travels east beneath the surface as a Kelvin wave.

  • A Kelvin wave crosses the Pacific in about 2 to 3 months, pushing the thermocline down as it goes.
  • When it reaches South America, upwelling brings up less-cold water and the sea warms.
  • Bjerknes feedback: a warmer east weakens the trades further, so the warming grows.

In 1969 Jacob Bjerknes explained how ocean and atmosphere reinforce each other. When the east–west temperature contrast shrinks, the Walker circulation weakens, the trades slacken and the contrast shrinks even more.

Why does it end? Ocean waves travelling west (Rossby waves) reflect off the western boundary and return as cold Kelvin waves. El Niño also drains the heat stored in the equatorial Pacific toward the poles. That is why the system oscillates instead of settling into one state.

Kelvin waves are tracked with moored buoys (the TAO/TRITON array) and with satellites that measure sea level: warm water takes up more volume.

El Niño, neutral and La Niña

The three phases

ENSO swings irregularly every 2 to 7 years between three states. During El Niño the central and eastern Pacific warm, the trades weaken and the rain moves east. La Niña is the opposite.

  • Events usually form between June and September and peak between November and January.
  • During El Niño the rain leaves Indonesia and Australia and settles over the central Pacific.
  • The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI) turns negative during El Niño and positive during La Niña.

An event is defined not by absolute warmth but by the anomaly: the difference from the average for that time of year. A "strong" El Niño can mean the sea is 2 °C warmer than normal in the Niño 3.4 region.

No two events are alike. In some the strongest warming sits in the central Pacific ("Modoki" or central-Pacific El Niño); in others it is next to South America. For Peru that difference is decisive, as the coastal El Niño lesson shows.

La Niña tends to last longer than El Niño and sometimes repeats for several years in a row, as in 2020–2023.

Coastal El Niño

Coastal El Niño

The sea off Peru can warm sharply even when the central Pacific does not, as in 1925, 2017 and early 2023. This is the coastal El Niño ("El Niño costero"), and Peru measures it with the ICEN (Coastal El Niño Index).

  • When the northern sea goes above about 26 °C, the air can build storms over a desert that normally gets no rain.
  • Heavy rain in Tumbes, Piura and Lambayeque; rivers overflow and huaicos (mudflows) rush down Andean ravines.
  • Anchoveta move south and deeper, and fishing seasons may be suspended.

The ICEN is the 3-month running mean of the sea temperature anomaly in the Niño 1+2 region (0°–10°S, 90°–80°W). Peru's ENFEN (the national El Niño study committee) uses it to declare a coastal El Niño and its magnitude: weak from +0.4 °C, moderate from +1.0, strong from +1.7 and extraordinary from +3.0 °C.

ENFEN issues Official Statements with an alert status: "inactive", "watch" or "alert". In 2017 a strong coastal El Niño affected more than a million people in Peru while the central Pacific stayed near neutral. 2015–16 was the reverse: a very strong central-Pacific El Niño with only moderate rain on the coast.

Other impacts include dengue outbreaks (a record in 2023) and losses in farming (rice, mango, lime) and infrastructure. In the southern Andes, by contrast, El Niño tends to bring drought to the Altiplano.

How it is measured

Regions and indices

Scientists watch sea temperature in four boxes along the equator. The anomaly in the Niño 3.4 region defines global events; the one in Niño 1+2, next to Peru and Ecuador, defines coastal El Niño.

  • ONI / RONI (NOAA): 3-month mean of Niño 3.4. El Niño starts at +0.5 °C, sustained over several seasons.
  • ICEN (ENFEN): 3-month mean of Niño 1+2, with its own thresholds for the Peruvian coast.
  • The values on the left are from the latest week (NOAA CPC).

Because the whole ocean is warming, NOAA now uses the Relative ONI (RONI), which compares Niño 3.4 with the average of the whole tropics. That keeps a globally warmer ocean from looking like a permanent El Niño.

The same number can mean different things: +1.5 °C is a "strong" El Niño for NOAA in Niño 3.4, but only "moderate" for ENFEN in Niño 1+2, because the coastal sea varies much more.

The regions: Niño 1+2 (0°–10°S, 90°–80°W), Niño 3 (5°N–5°S, 150°–90°W), Niño 3.4 (5°N–5°S, 170°–120°W) and Niño 4 (5°N–5°S, 160°E–150°W).

Impacts around the world

Teleconnections

By moving tropical rain thousands of kilometres, El Niño reshapes the circulation of the whole atmosphere. These teleconnections change rainfall and temperature far from the Pacific. They are tendencies, not certainties: every event is different.

  • Wetter: the Peru and Ecuador coast, southeastern South America, the southern US, East Africa.
  • Drier: Indonesia, Australia, southern Africa, northern South America, the Indian monsoon.
  • The planet warms: the years after a strong El Niño often set records, like 2016 and 2024.

Heavy tropical rain releases heat into the atmosphere. When that heat source shifts to the central Pacific, it sends atmospheric waves toward the mid-latitudes that bend the jet streams.

Other typical effects: fewer Atlantic hurricanes (stronger wind shear), more central and eastern Pacific cyclones, fires in Indonesia and the Amazon, and mass coral bleaching (1998, 2010, 2016 and 2023–2025).

A century of El Niños

History

Each column is one year: the Niño 3.4 anomaly (ONI) in December–February, the usual peak. Warm columns are El Niños and cool ones La Niñas. The strong events stand out: 1982–83, 1997–98, 2015–16 and 2023–24.

  • Very strong El Niños (≥ +2.0 °C) come roughly once a decade.
  • For Peru the most devastating were 1982–83 and 1997–98, plus the coastal events of 1925 and 2017.
  • The last column is the current event, using the latest value.

Before 1950 there are fewer measurements, but historical and paleoclimate records (corals, sediments, colonial chronicles) show that El Niño has existed for thousands of years.

Is global warming changing El Niño? The question is still open. Several models project more extreme rainfall in the eastern Pacific during El Niño, but there is no consensus on how often events will occur. What is clear is that an El Niño on top of a warmer ocean pushes global temperatures to records more easily.

2015–16. Very strong in the central Pacific, with moderate impacts on Peru's coast. Fires in Indonesia; 2016 set a heat record.
